# Chapter 3: Make the Abstract Concrete

Three days after the team focus meeting, Nia got invited to present at a customer advisory breakfast hosted in the back room of a downtown hotel.

This sounded flattering until she learned what it actually meant.

Eight operations leaders. Two finance people. One investor who had backed a warehouse software company and now had opinions about every workflow product in North America. Coffee in paper cups. Dry eggs. Low ceilings. Bad microphones. The kind of room where nobody wants a speech but everyone has been trapped inside one before.

The host introduced Nia as "a founder building next-generation orchestration for distributed logistics environments."

Nia felt the phrase land on the room like wet cardboard.

She had written that description herself.

It had seemed intelligent at midnight.

Now she watched a dispatch director blink twice and look down at her plate. The investor crossed one ankle over the other with the slow composure of a man preparing to hear nonsense at professional volume.

Nia opened her slides.

On the first screen was a clean diagram of nodes, arrows, event triggers, rule groups, and exception states. It was technically correct. Marcus had helped refine it. The product team liked it because it made the system look substantial and thoughtfully engineered.

It also looked like something only the product team would ever enjoy.

"What we have built," Nia began, "is a flexible operational layer that normalizes multi-site dispatch logic across variable local environments."

There are sentences that communicate an idea.

There are sentences that attempt to hide the absence of one.

This was the second kind.

Nia kept talking. She explained unified rule execution, configurable exception handling, modular intake states, and the importance of preserving local variance without sacrificing system visibility.

No one interrupted, which is often a bad sign.

The dispatch director looked polite and unconvinced. The finance manager narrowed her eyes in the way people do when they are trying to decide whether confusion is their fault or yours. The investor took a sip of coffee and looked past Nia at nothing in particular, which is one of the more discouraging forms of listening.

Then a woman from a regional carrier raised her hand.

"Can I ask a dumb question?"

"Please."

"What does this actually do when one site routes hot shipments by customer tier and another site routes them by driver territory and both teams think their rule is the obvious one?"

It was not a dumb question. It was the first real question in the room.

Nia answered with sincerity and made the problem worse.

"The system abstracts the routing logic into a shared framework," she said, "so each site maintains rule specificity while leadership gets cross-network consistency."

The woman nodded slowly, the way a person nods when she is deciding to stop asking.

The investor finally looked at Nia directly.

"So if I understand correctly," he said, "you have built software that does something useful, and you are describing it in a dialect spoken only by software."

The room laughed.

Not cruelly. Worse. With relief.

Because he had said what everyone else had been too disciplined to say.

## The Curse of Knowing Too Much

After the breakfast, Nia walked back to the office with the sensation that her own vocabulary had betrayed her.

Marcus was waiting near the whiteboard, untangling a charger from his backpack with the focus of a man working on a bomb.

"How did it go?"

"I was introduced as next-generation orchestration."

He looked up.

"That is not an answer."

"It went badly in a very professional way."

She set her laptop on the table and replayed the meeting for him. The hotel room. The dead diagram. The investor. The question about hot shipments. The sentence about abstractions and frameworks that had caused the room to leave her without physically moving.

Marcus listened, then said, "You were speaking from inside the product."

"Where else would I speak from?"

"From the customer's situation."

This annoyed Nia because it was both simple and correct.

Technical people often believe detail is proof. They have spent so long earning their understanding that they cannot imagine any honorable way to discuss a system without displaying its internal parts. They know how difficult the work was. They know which tradeoffs mattered. They know what broke three times before the architecture stopped embarrassing itself. They know which data model saved the whole project and which edge case nearly set fire to the roadmap.

This knowledge is valuable.

It is also dangerous when carried straight into speech.

The speaker who knows too much often starts in the middle.

They begin with architecture before outcome. Mechanism before pain. Vocabulary before context. They hand the audience a pile of concepts and call it clarity because they themselves can see how the pieces fit.

But the audience cannot see inside the speaker's head.

They need a door.

That is why abstract speaking fails. It assumes the listener will perform the translation labor privately and in real time.

Most people will not.

Not because they are lazy.

Because they are busy.

A buyer is thinking about risk. A finance lead is thinking about cost and failure modes. A team member is thinking about workload and status. An investor is thinking about whether this person understands the market or merely the product. A customer is wondering whether the promised improvement will survive contact with the chaos of Tuesday afternoon.

If the speaker gives them only abstraction, each listener must invent the concrete version alone.

Some will invent it poorly.

Some will invent it against you.

Many will stop trying.

## Show Me Tuesday at 4:17

That afternoon, Nia called Ivy Chen, a customer success lead who had become the company's unofficial translator between code and commerce.

Ivy had a rare talent. She could listen to an engineer explain a system for seven minutes, ask two calm questions, and then summarize the same idea in language a tired operations manager could understand before taking a second sip of coffee.

Nia told her what happened.

Ivy did not laugh, which was considerate.

"What did you show them first?" Ivy asked.

"The rules engine."

"No. What did you show them first?"

"A diagram of the rules engine."

"Still no."

Nia leaned back in her chair.

"Fine. What should I have shown them first?"

Ivy said, "Show me Tuesday at 4:17."

"What?"

"Show me the moment the customer actually cares about. Not the platform. Not the architecture. The moment."

Nia waited.

Ivy continued.

"At 4:17 p.m., what is happening in the customer's world when your product matters?"

That question cleared the fog at once.

Tuesday at 4:17, one warehouse had a driver call in sick. Another site had a priority shipment for a customer who penalized late arrivals. The dispatcher was already covering two exceptions, finance needed clean billing codes by close, and everyone was routing decisions through whichever experienced employee happened to still be at a desk.

That was the moment.

That was the thing the software was for.

Ivy said, "Start there. If I can see the moment, I can care about the system. If you start with the system, I have to guess why the moment matters."

This is the central rule of concrete speaking:

Move from scene to principle, not principle to fog.

People understand what they can picture.

A late shipment.

A confused buyer.

A manager stuck in a status meeting because nobody trusts the dashboard.

A founder losing a room because the explanation arrived as a cloud instead of a path.

Concrete speech does not mean childish speech. It does not mean oversimplifying until the truth becomes soft and useless. It means giving the mind something it can hold while the deeper idea takes shape.

The abstract idea may be accurate.

The concrete example makes it portable.

## Example, Story, Demo, Analogy

Nia wrote four words across the whiteboard:

**Example. Story. Demo. Analogy.**

These were not decoration. They were tools.

An example says, "Here is one specific case."

A story says, "Here is change over time."

A demo says, "Watch it happen."

An analogy says, "Borrow understanding from something you already know."

Most strong explanations use more than one.

The weak speaker says, "Our platform creates operational visibility."

The stronger speaker says, "Yesterday a dispatch lead had to call three people to learn why a priority load was late. With this system, she sees the exception, the cause, and the next owner in one place."

That is an example.

The weak speaker says, "The product enables standardized flexibility."

The stronger speaker says, "One customer had grown site by site for ten years. Every warehouse invented its own workaround. The software did not erase those differences overnight. It gave them one place to capture which differences mattered and which ones were just inherited chaos."

That is a story.

The weak speaker says, "The rules are configurable."

The stronger speaker says, "Let me show you one shipment entering from Site A, then watch how the routing changes when the same shipment enters Site B with different rules and staffing."

That is a demo.

The weak speaker says, "The platform serves as a control layer."

The stronger speaker says, "Think of it as air traffic control for exceptions. The planes are different, the routes vary, weather changes, priorities change, but everybody still needs one reliable way to see what is delayed, what is urgent, and who is clearing the path."

That is an analogy.

The analogy is not the product. It is a bridge.

Bad speakers either refuse bridges or live on them too long.

If the analogy becomes more vivid than the actual point, it starts stealing from the truth. But when used carefully, it lets a listener borrow a familiar shape and carry it toward a new idea.

Nia saw her mistake clearly now. She had been speaking as if precision required abstraction. Often the opposite is true. The more important the idea, the more carefully it should be tied to a scene, a choice, a person, a before and after.

## Make the Listener Do Less Work

Two days later, Nia had another chance.

This one mattered more than the hotel breakfast.

A prospective customer from a national field-service company wanted a working session. They were not buying yet. They were deciding whether Nia's team understood the messy middle between "interesting software" and "operational change that survives real people."

Before the call, Marcus reviewed Nia's new opening slide.

It had no diagram.

It had five lines:

At 4:17 p.m. your best dispatcher is covering three exceptions.

A priority customer wants an answer now.

One site routes by territory. Another routes by service level.

Finance still needs a clean billing trail before close.

You do not have a software problem first. You have a decision problem.

Marcus read the slide twice.

"This is much better," he said. "I hate that it is much better."

"You hate clarity?"

"I hate how long we needed to deserve it."

On the call, Nia began with the scene, not the system.

"I want to start with a moment you already know," she said. "Late afternoon. Two urgent exceptions at once. The person who usually untangles them is already buried. Everybody is improvising, and if the decision gets made wrong or late, the problem shows up again in billing, customer trust, and management visibility."

Heads nodded.

No one had to translate.

Then she told a short story about a regional operator who had grown through acquisition and inherited five different rule sets nobody trusted enough to document cleanly. She showed one example workflow from intake to dispatch to invoice handoff. She demonstrated how the same exception moved differently under two site policies without disappearing into manual memory. When a finance lead asked what leadership actually gained from that visibility, Nia answered with an analogy:

"Right now your managers often learn about operational fires through smoke. This gives them a panel that shows heat before it becomes flame."

Not perfect.

Useful.

The room stayed with her.

This is what concrete language does. It lowers processing cost.

The audience no longer has to spend its energy turning your abstractions into reality. That energy can go toward judging the idea itself.

This matters in every kind of speaking.

If you are explaining product strategy, show the specific customer choice that forced the strategy.

If you are pitching investors, show the repeated painful workflow that creates demand.

If you are leading a team, show the exact bottleneck everyone keeps stepping around.

If you are teaching a technical concept, show the failure case before the framework.

Abstraction has a place. General principles matter. Categories matter. Models matter.

But abstraction should arrive after understanding begins, not before.

The mind climbs by steps.

Do not hand it a ladder made of mist.

## The Test of the Kitchen Table

Later that week, Nia visited her brother Adrian, who ran a small appliance repair business and had no professional interest in software architecture.

This made him useful.

She told him about the breakfast meeting and her new slide.

Adrian listened while replacing a cracked switch on a toaster oven for reasons Nia had long ago stopped trying to classify.

"So what does your product do?" he asked.

Nia started to answer, stopped, and tried again.

"It helps operations teams make the right exception decisions faster when each location has different rules and nobody can afford to route work through tribal memory."

Adrian looked at her.

"That is closer," he said. "Try it so I can explain it to someone else."

This is another good test of clarity.

Can someone outside your field repeat the idea without injuring it?

Nia thought for a moment.

"It gives busy teams one place to see what is going wrong, what rule applies, and what should happen next when normal process breaks."

Adrian nodded.

"That I can carry."

Carry.

That was the word.

Good speech is easier to carry.

People leave the room able to repeat it, use it, and apply it. They may not remember every supporting fact, but they remember the shape. They remember the scene. They remember the line that made the invisible thing visible.

Many speakers lose influence because they make the listener hold too much weight too early. They front-load terms, caveats, and categories because they are afraid of being imprecise. In doing so, they become unusable.

Precision is not the same as density.

A sentence can be exact and still be human.

## Let Them See It

The next month, Nia returned to the customer advisory group for a shorter follow-up.

Same low ceiling. Same coffee. Same investor.

This time she opened differently.

"At your busiest sites, the most expensive work often hides inside exceptions. A hot shipment changes priority, a driver disappears, a billing code does not follow the route, and suddenly your best people are solving the same kind of problem from scratch. We built this for that moment."

Then she showed one screen.

One workflow.

One before.

One after.

The dispatch director from the first breakfast interrupted after four minutes.

"Now I get it," he said. "You are not trying to standardize every site. You are trying to stop every exception from becoming folklore."

Nia smiled.

"Exactly."

He did not invent that sentence from nothing. She had given him enough concrete material to build it.

That is the task.

Not merely to speak.

To make seeing possible.

When an idea is abstract, the audience keeps it at arm's length. When the idea is concrete, they can test it against experience. They can agree, object, compare, remember, and repeat. That is when speech begins to do work.

The speaker who stays abstract may sound sophisticated for a minute.

The speaker who makes the thing visible gets believed.

So before you explain any difficult idea, ask:

What is the scene?

What is the example?

What is the smallest useful story?

What can I show?

What familiar thing can help carry the meaning without distorting it?

Then begin there.

Do not ask the room to admire your vocabulary.

Give the room something it can see.

People trust what becomes visible.
