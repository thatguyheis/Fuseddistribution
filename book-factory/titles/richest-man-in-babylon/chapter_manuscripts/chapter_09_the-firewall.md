# Chapter 9: The Walls of the Valley

Old Banzar had worked in cybersecurity for thirty years, and he had never given a TED talk.

Not glamorous work. No viral LinkedIn posts. No speaking circuit. He had spent a decade as a network security engineer, then another as a CISO for a midsize enterprise, and now, semi-retired but never fully quiet, he consulted for organizations that needed someone who had seen everything and panicked about nothing. These organizations paid extremely well for that quality.

He had been hired by the Valley's largest platform company to oversee their infrastructure defense during what the internal memos were calling a "sustained coordinated intrusion attempt." Less politely: someone very sophisticated was trying very hard to get in.

Banzar set up his command center in a glass-walled monitoring room. His team worked in shifts. On his screens: live traffic, anomaly alerts, endpoint status, real-time threat vectors from a dozen different attack surfaces. He drank bad coffee and watched numbers scroll and felt, as he always did in these situations, a strange calm.

Around him, staff circulated constantly, engineers, product managers, executives, and the occasional PR person trying to understand how to frame "we are under attack" for an audience that didn't understand what that meant.

A senior product manager stopped by, visibly rattled. "Is our data safe? My whole team's work is on these servers. Years of roadmaps, user data, everything."

"The firewall holds," Banzar said. "Go back to your desk."

A junior engineer lingered nervously by the door. "They keep hitting the API gateway. What happens if they get through?"

"They won't. The protocols are layered. Go build something."

A head of marketing appeared, wringing her hands. "Our users are going to lose trust if this gets out. What do I tell them?"

"Tell them the walls protect them. They do. That's why we built them."

For five days the attacks continued. State-level sophistication. Multiple vectors. Patient and methodical. Banzar did not sleep much. His team rotated. Alerts fired and were resolved. Escalations were met. Each attempted breach was logged, analyzed, blocked. The attackers were excellent. They kept finding new angles. Banzar's team kept closing them.

On day three a young engineer named Nila made the mistake every young engineer makes at least once under pressure. She suggested, half apologetically, that maybe they should loosen one of the internal access rules temporarily because it was slowing the response workflow.

Banzar turned from the monitor slowly enough that everyone in the room understood a lesson was coming.

"When people are scared," he said, "they always want to weaken the controls that are annoying them. That is true in a breach. That is true in finance. That is true in life generally. The rule that feels inconvenient in a crisis is usually the rule that exists because of crises."

Nila nodded, chastened but grateful.

"You don't remove the lock because the burglar is making noise," Banzar said. "You check the hinges."

After that, no one suggested weakening anything.

On the morning of the sixth day, the attack stopped.

Traffic normalized. The anomaly flags went quiet. Banzar sat back in his chair and drank a very bad cup of vending machine coffee like it was the finest espresso in Northern California.

His team cheered. He nodded once, the way old soldiers nod when a battle ends. Not with relief. With professional satisfaction.

The system held because it had been built to hold. Not improvised in a crisis. Built before the crisis. Layers of defense. Redundancy. Continuous monitoring. An incident response plan that had been tested and tested again long before anyone needed it. When the attack came, there was nothing to figure out. They just executed the plan.

---

He was asked to give a brief talk to the all-hands that afternoon.

He stood at the front of the room with the slightly uncomfortable posture of a man who preferred being behind a screen.

"We held," he said. "Why did we hold? Not because our attackers were bad. They were excellent. Not because we were lucky. Luck is not a security strategy.

"We held because the walls were built before the attack. The encryption was in place. The detection systems were running. The response protocols were rehearsed. When the attack came, we had nothing to improvise. We executed the plan.

"The same principle applies to your personal financial life. I know that's an odd thing to say at a security debrief, but stay with me.

"The people who made it through the 2008 financial crisis intact were not the people who scrambled when the crash came. They were the ones who had built protection before the crash: diversified holdings, emergency funds, no overexposure to a single asset class.

"The people who held through the crypto winter of 2022 without losing everything were not the ones who panicked at the bottom. They had positioned defensibly before the winter. Only invested what they could afford to lose. Held real assets alongside speculative ones. Maintained liquidity. They did not store their entire net worth on one exchange. They had their seed phrases written down somewhere safe and their cold storage wallets set up long before they needed to move anything in a hurry.

"A firewall doesn't help you when the attack is already inside your network. A rainy-day fund doesn't help you after you've already taken on debt to cover the emergency. The protection has to be in place before you need it.

"Insurance. Diversification. Emergency savings. A will. Beneficiary designations on your crypto wallets. These are boring. Nobody gets excited about them. But they are the walls that protect everything you've built when the unexpected comes. And the unexpected always comes."

He looked around the room and could tell which people were hearing him as a metaphor and which were hearing him as a warning.

"Some of you make very good money," he continued. "That does not impress risk. A layoff does not care what your title was. A medical bill does not care that you were about to vest. A market drawdown does not care that your last three years felt like genius. The walls are not for pessimists. They are for adults."

He paused.

"We cannot afford to be without adequate protection. Not in cybersecurity. Not in personal finance. Build the walls before you need them."

He stepped off the platform to mild but genuine applause.

Later, one of the junior engineers found him at the coffee machine.

"You really compared cybersecurity to personal finance?"

"They're the same problem," Banzar said. "Threat modeling. Defense in depth. Regular audits. Incident response. The principles are identical. The consequences of failure are similarly unpleasant."

"I've actually been meaning to ask you something," the engineer said. "My financial planner keeps telling me to build an emergency fund before I do anything interesting, and I keep not doing it because it feels... slow."

Banzar stirred powdered creamer into his coffee with the grim concentration of someone performing a task beneath his dignity.

"Then you are making the same mistake companies make when they delay security spending until after a breach," he said. "You think the boring thing is optional because the disaster has not yet become theatrical."

"They've been saying it's safe for months. Meanwhile I don't have six hundred bucks for a real emergency. I've got a brokerage account but I keep pulling money out of it every time life gets weird. Which is often."

"Then you have a wall made of cardboard," Banzar said.

The engineer laughed, then realized Banzar was not joking.

"So what do I do?"

"Three things first. Build one month of living expenses in cash. Stop pretending a brokerage account is an emergency fund if you liquidate it every time your feelings change. And insure the obvious risks. Health. Disability if people depend on your income. Term life if you have kids. You don't install enterprise-grade protection by buying a cool lock for the front door and leaving the windows open."

"That's less exciting than I hoped."

"Of course it is," Banzar said. "Security done right is boring. That is one of its chief virtues."

"Did you always think like this?"

"No. I thought like this after I watched a colleague lose half his retirement because all of it was in the stock of the company that laid him off. Smart man. Excellent engineer. No defense in depth. One breach and the whole system went down."

He picked up the coffee and took a sip that disappointed him immediately.

"People think protection is fear. It isn't. It's respect for the fact that life has threat surfaces."

He poured his coffee.

"Build the walls. Then you can sleep."
