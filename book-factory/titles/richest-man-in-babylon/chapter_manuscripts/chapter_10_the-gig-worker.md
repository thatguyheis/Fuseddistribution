# Chapter 10: The Gig Worker Who Found His Soul

The hungrier you get, the clearer your thinking.

Tark knew this from personal experience. He had not eaten anything substantial in two days, a protein bar from a 7-Eleven the morning before, and a small bag of chips he'd bummed from a coworker. His stomach had stopped growling and was now sending complaints directly to his central nervous system in a quieter, more insistent way.

He walked through a food hall in downtown San Francisco with the particular suffering of someone who can smell everything and afford nothing. The ramen smelled incredible. The tacos looked life-changingly good. Even the overpriced acai bowl with its aggressive array of seeds seemed, right now, like the food of the gods.

No card with available balance. His Venmo balance was $1.40.

He was twenty-nine years old, had a computer science degree, and had spent the last three years in a spiral of lifestyle creep, poor financial choices, deferred payment apps, and the kind of optimistic borrowing from friends that eventually makes you the person everyone avoids at parties.

He spotted, coming out of a Vietnamese place across the hall, the one person he most needed to avoid: Dazz.

Dazz was tall, cheerful, dressed simply well, and had a memory for debts that would have impressed a bank.

"Tark!" Dazz said, with the warmth of someone who genuinely liked you and also wanted their money back. "Been looking for you. The Venmo request has been pending for six weeks. The two hundred bucks, plus the forty from before that. You good for it?"

Tark's face said everything.

Dazz studied him for a moment. Then: "When did you last eat?"

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. Come in, let me buy you some food. And then I'm going to tell you a story."

---

They found a corner table. Dazz ordered generously, a full bowl of pho, spring rolls, a juice, and watched Tark try not to look as relieved as he felt.

"I'm not going to lecture you," Dazz said. "But I am going to tell you something true. Because I recognize what you look like right now. I looked exactly the same once."

He leaned back.

"You know what I do now? I run a logistics tech startup. We manage last-mile delivery coordination for three regional carriers. Twelve employees, cash flow positive. I'm not rich rich, but I'm solid. I sleep well. I have savings. I own a small piece of a real estate syndication. I've been dollar-cost averaging into Bitcoin for four years, small amounts, every month, no matter what the price is doing, because I'm not trying to time it, I'm trying to hold it.

"You know what I did at your age? I was a DoorDash driver who had somehow accumulated forty-three thousand dollars in credit card debt."

Tark looked up from the pho.

"Forty-three thousand?"

"Forty-three. I had the soul of a slave and didn't know it."

---

Dazz told the story the way people tell the stories that changed their lives, with enough detail to make it real and enough distance to make it instructive rather than painful.

He had grown up in logistics, his family ran a small freight business, but when the gig economy took off, he had decided the easy money was in driving. Good money at first. But he spent as fast as he earned. Better apartment. New phone every year. The best sneakers. Clothes he didn't need. Restaurants three nights a week. His friends borrowed from him because he always seemed to have money, and he lent because it felt good to be generous and terrible to say no.

Then the work slowed down. Competition in the gig economy had compressed his margins. He had no savings to buffer against the slowdown. The credit cards he'd been supplementing his income with started to feel like walls closing in. He borrowed from friends to pay bills. Then borrowed from other friends to pay back the first friends. Then avoided everyone because the mental accounting was too exhausting.

"I left San Francisco," Dazz said. "I told myself it was because there were better opportunities elsewhere. The real reason was that I was running away from debts I couldn't face. I spent two years in that kind of fugue state. Working gigs in different cities. Lying to myself that I'd deal with the debt later. Later never came."

He stopped. Took a sip of his drink. Outside the food hall, the city moved past without pausing.

"I ended up doing Amazon Flex routes in Phoenix. The desert heat. Twelve-hour days. One summer it was so bad the asphalt shimmered even at five in the morning. I was exhausted and humiliated and completely out of ideas.

"One morning I sat in the parking lot of a warehouse at five-thirty am. Nothing dramatic. Just me and a parking lot and the specific kind of quiet you get in a desert city before the heat starts. And I had what I can only describe as a moment of clarity.

"I thought: I keep blaming bad luck. Bad economy. Bad city. Bad platform algorithms. But the only constant in every version of my life where I was broke is me.

"And then I thought about my debts. Not as enemies, exactly, but as creditors I had let down. People who had trusted me. And I realized that running from them wasn't freedom. It was its own kind of slavery. As long as I was avoiding those conversations, those people, those numbers, I was owned by them. The debt was my master and I was its gig worker.

"The soul of a free man faces his debts. The soul of a slave runs from them.

"That morning I decided: I was going back. I was going to face every single one."

---

He returned to San Francisco. It was not comfortable. He had awkward calls. One old friend he'd borrowed a few hundred dollars from had a lot of feelings about it. But most people, most people, were surprisingly understanding when he laid it out honestly. *Here is what I owe you. I cannot pay it all today. But I am going to pay it systematically, a percentage of everything I earn, until it is gone. I need you to be patient.*

He found a day job at a logistics company. Started saving ten percent off the top, his first financial rule, the first one he'd ever applied consistently. Allocated twenty percent to debt repayment, distributed proportionally across everyone he owed. Lived on the remaining seventy percent, tightly, without complaining.

One of his creditors, a former manager at the logistics company, had seen the change in him and offered him a project management role. That role became a full-time job. That job gave him enough stability to start building something on the side. The side thing became the startup.

"Four years," Dazz said. "That's how long it took from the parking lot in Phoenix to paying off the last debt and having twenty thousand dollars in savings. Four years of boring, consistent, unglamorous execution. No shortcuts. No windfalls. Just the plan."

He looked at Tark steadily.

"Your situation is not hopeless. I know it feels like it is. The debt doesn't disappear by itself. Neither does the hole. You have to decide to climb out of it. And climbing out starts with one decision: I am going to face every number, talk to every person, make a plan, and execute it."

Tark was quiet for a long time.

"What if they're angry?"

"Some will be. Deal with it. Their anger is proportional to how long you've avoided the conversation. The avoidance is what makes it worse. The conversation, as uncomfortable as it is, makes it better."

"What if I can't pay them back as fast as they want?"

"Show them a credible plan. Most people would rather receive a portion of something reliably than all of nothing indefinitely."

Tark stared at the table. Something was shifting behind his eyes. Some calculation that had been running in the background for years was finally returning a result.

"I think I've been running from myself," he said.

"Yeah," said Dazz. "But here's the good news about running from yourself. You're always right there when you decide to stop."

He pushed the check across the table.

"Come back and pay me when you're squared up. And then let me introduce you to some people who can help you build something real."

---

Three years later, Tark showed up at Dazz's office. A small but real office in SoMa, with a team of four and a growing client list. He sat in the same kind of plastic chair he'd sat in at the food hall, only now he sat in it differently.

He placed an envelope on the desk.

Dazz opened it. Inside was a check for the full amount owed, plus interest that nobody had asked for, plus a handwritten note that said only: *Thank you for not pretending I wasn't there.*

"Tell me something," Dazz said. "How'd it feel, paying the last one?"

Tark thought about it.

"Like being unlocked," he said. "Like I could walk down the street and look at anyone."
