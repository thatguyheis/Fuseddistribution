# Chapter 3: The Dev Who Wanted Bitcoin

Bansir, the app developer, was in a mood.

He sat on the front steps of his rented townhouse in San Jose, three bedrooms, two and a half baths, landlord who raised the rent every single year with the cheerfulness of someone who had never once worried about rent in their life. From his seat on the stoop, Bansir stared at the unfinished MVP on his laptop and felt the particular despair of a man who was technically employed but somehow still broke.

His girlfriend had already peeked out the front door twice to remind him, in the kind of voice that meant something much larger, that the credit card bill was overdue. Again. He should have been inside coding. His client wanted the app finished by Friday. But his brain had gone somewhere his hands could not follow.

The sun was doing that thing where it lingers too long over the Bay Area in early October, warm and indifferent, as if the universe itself had no concept of a looming payment deadline.

He had two side projects going. One was an AI scheduling tool he'd been building with a friend, a simple agent that handled appointment booking for local service businesses. The other was a prompt-engineering course he kept meaning to finish. Neither had made him a dollar yet, because he kept running out of hours. Sixty hours a week went to client work that paid his rent. The side projects existed in the margins. The margins kept shrinking.

Inside his laptop bag were three unopened envelopes. One from the credit card company. One from the California tax board. One from a dentist in Santa Clara who had sent his bill to collections with stunning speed and surprisingly good typography. Bansir knew approximately what each envelope contained and had been conducting the kind of childish negotiation with reality that adults sometimes conduct under stress: if he did not open them tonight, perhaps tomorrow's version of them would be kinder.

They would not.

On the kitchen counter inside, his girlfriend had left a legal pad with numbers on it. Rent. Utilities. Card minimums. Car insurance. Software subscriptions he insisted were essential and rarely used. The total at the bottom had been underlined twice, the second underline digging hard enough into the paper to leave an imprint on the next page.

This, more than anything, offended him. Not the number itself. The shape of it. The fact that he could build things people praised, solve technical problems other people found impossible, and still be defeated by a legal pad and a sum in blue ink.

Down the street rolled a gleaming white Tesla Model S, the kind with the full autonomous driving package and the custom wheels. Bansir watched it coast past with the quiet humiliation of a man who had been telling people he would "definitely have a Tesla by thirty." He was thirty-two.

A familiar ringtone snapped him back. His friend Kobbi had set himself as a contact with some snippet of a beat he'd been working on. It cut through everything.

Kobbi the music producer was at the door, grinning.

"What's good, man? You out here looking like someone just rugged your NFT collection."

"Because they did," said Bansir, which was not entirely untrue. He moved over on the steps. "Sit down. I need to tell you something."

Kobbi sat, still in his AirPods, still vibing faintly to something only he could hear. He was a talented producer, genuinely talented, the kind who had worked with people whose names you'd actually recognize. But the streaming era had not been kind to the economics of music. He had a decent following on SoundCloud, a few sync licenses, and an empty savings account.

Kobbi had the look many modern talented people had: expensive taste, real skill, and no durable margin. His shoes were immaculate. His bank balance, Bansir knew, was not. He had once gotten a five-figure check for a placement and spent most of it over four months in the kind of soft, ambient lifestyle creep that never feels dramatic while it is happening. Studio upgrades. Dinners. Ride shares. Gear. A weekend in Miami. Then nothing. The money had left as quietly as it came.

"We're the same," Bansir said.

"The same how?"

"We're both good at what we do. We've been working hard for years. We're not dumb. We went to school, leveled up our skills, we're out here grinding." He gestured at the world. "And we're both completely, utterly broke."

Kobbi pulled out one AirPod. "I mean. Yeah."

"It bothers me. Really bothers me." Bansir closed the laptop. "I had a dream last night where my crypto wallet had seven figures in it. I woke up and checked it and it had forty-three dollars and some ETH I forgot I bought in 2021."

"Dude. That ETH is down sixty percent."

"I know that."

Bansir rubbed his eyes. "I don't even think I want luxury, not really. I want breathing room. I want to stop feeling like every client payment is an oxygen refill. I want to stop doing that thing where the invoice clears and for twelve minutes I feel like a serious adult and then every bill in my life swarms it like piranhas."

Kobbi nodded in a way that made it clear he knew the feeling exactly.

"Last month," Kobbi said, "I got paid for a sync placement. Good money. I thought, finally, amazing, I can catch up. By the end of the week it was gone. Rent, card, producer software, paying back my cousin, and then I bought dinner twice because I was depressed and apparently my coping mechanism is truffle fries."

"Truffle fries are the language of financial self-destruction," Bansir said.

"They really are."

They sat with that for a moment.

"I keep seeing guys we went to school with," Bansir continued. "Same grades as us. Same background. Some of them weren't even as good at their thing as we are at ours. But they've got portfolios. Properties. The kind of net worth that means they could take six months off and not panic."

He did not say the part he was actually thinking, which was that he had begun to feel suspicious around people who were calm. Anyone under thirty-five with a steady expression now seemed to him either secretly rich, secretly medicated, or possessed of some money system he had failed to download. He himself had become too familiar with a particular low-level panic, the kind that never rises high enough to look dramatic but hums through every decision: what can I delay, what can I pay, what can I pretend not to know for three more days.

"You're talking about Arkad Morrow," Kobbi said.

Bansir blinked. "I wasn't going to say his name."

"But you were thinking it."

He was thinking it. Arkad Morrow, a name that had become almost mythic in their circle because the man had an almost supernatural relationship with money and also a personality that filled whatever room he was in. Arkad had gone to the same community college as both of them for a semester before transferring. He had started three companies, two of which had been acquired. He'd held Bitcoin since 2013, back when one coin was a few hundred dollars, and had never once panic-sold, not through the 2018 crash, not through the brutal 2022 bear market when half the industry lost everything. He was now, by most estimates, one of the wealthiest private citizens in the greater San Francisco Bay Area. He was also, to both Bansir and Kobbi's certain knowledge, not any smarter than either of them.

That was the part that stung.

Because if Arkad had been obviously extraordinary, Bansir could have protected himself with a story. Genius, family money, luck, connections, some personality quirk unavailable to ordinary men. But Arkad was annoyingly legible. He was sharp, yes, but not mythically so. He remembered people's names. He asked follow-up questions. He bought his own coffee. He seemed, in the most irritating possible way, like someone who had simply learned things Bansir had not.

"Do you think it's luck?" Kobbi asked.

"I used to think so." Bansir looked down at the concrete. "But I've been watching him for years and luck doesn't explain all of it. The man has a system. He has a way of thinking about money that we don't."

"So what do we do about it?"

Bansir looked up. "We ask him."

Kobbi raised an eyebrow. "We just... ask the richest man in the Valley how he got rich?"

"He's not like that. You know he's not. Last week I saw him at Blue Bottle and he came over and we talked for twenty minutes. Didn't look over my head once."

"He bought his own coffee," Kobbi added, remembering. "Stood in line like everyone else."

"That's him. That's the thing about him." Bansir stood up, energy crackling through the decision. "He's going to help us. I know it. And let's bring Marcus and Pete and whoever else from the old days who's out here grinding and has nothing to show for it. Because this is bigger than us."

He said it with more certainty than he felt, but the certainty improved as he heard himself say it. That happens sometimes. Speech makes courage available a few seconds before belief catches up. What he knew was that continuing exactly as he had been was no longer tolerable. The numbers on the legal pad had seen to that. Something had to break: the system or the man. He preferred the system.

"What are you going to say to him?"

Bansir picked up his laptop. "The truth. That we're good at what we do. That we've worked hard. That we've somehow managed to live in the richest tech ecosystem on the planet and not hold any of its wealth. Then I'm going to ask him to tell us how he did it."

Kobbi stood, already reaching for his phone to text the group chat.

"You always were good at talking to people, Bansir. That's why you're going to make it."

"We," Bansir said. "We're going to make it."

**And so they went, together, to find the richest man in the Valley, and to ask him the question everyone wants answered but almost no one dares to ask.**
