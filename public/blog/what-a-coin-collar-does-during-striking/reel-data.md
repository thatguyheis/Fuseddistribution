# What a Coin Collar Does During Striking

hook: The edge has its own tool

## segments
- type: overlay
 text: LOCATE THE COLLAR
 narration: The Mint explains that the two dies press against a planchet while a collar surrounds it. The collar controls outward movement and can create a smooth, reeded or lettered edge. This is why an explanation of the two face dies alone leaves out part of the tooling involved in producing a coin.
- type: overlay
 text: SEPARATE THE SURFACES
 narration: When looking at a coin, describe which surface you mean before describing its appearance. A photograph taken straight at a face may show little of the edge. An angled view can introduce shadows that make a surface look more irregular than it is. Record the view and lighting alongside your observation.
- type: overlay
 text: CHECK THE PROCESS
 narration: Do not assume every edge inscription is made during the main strike. The Mint describes dollar coins receiving a smooth edge in the collar before a separate machine applies lettering. That exception is a good reason to look up the process for the actual product rather than extending a general diagram to every coin.
- type: overlay
 text: DESCRIBE BEFORE DIAGNOSING
 narration: If an edge interests you, begin with neutral observations and clear photographs. Note where the feature appears and whether it continues around the circumference. Avoid cleaning or altering the object to make a theory easier to photograph. Keep existing packaging and identifiers with the record so later review can use the original context. The useful takeaway is the division of work: the dies address the faces, while the collar surrounds the metal during striking and helps establish the edge. Use that distinction to ask better questions about a documented production process. Leave product identification, error attribution and valuation outside the claim unless you have evidence for those separate conclusions.
