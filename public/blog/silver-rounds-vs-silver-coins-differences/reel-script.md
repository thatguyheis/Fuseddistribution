## HOOK

**Visual:** Silver coins and rounds side by side, close-up
**Duration:** 20s minimum (46 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: Silver rounds and silver coins look identical. Both weigh one troy ounce. Both contain .999 fine silver. The only difference is who made them, and that one fact changes your cost by 15 to 20 percent. Here is what you need to know before you buy.

---

## STAT: 3-8 PERCENT ROUND PREMIUM

**Visual:** Private mint silver rounds in tubes
**Duration:** 22s minimum (50 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: Generic silver rounds from private mints carry the lowest premiums of any one-ounce silver product. You pay 3 to 8 percent over spot price. No government program costs baked in. No authorized dealer network adding margin. No collector premium built in. You are buying pure silver at near-spot pricing.

---

## STAT: 15-25 PERCENT EAGLE PREMIUM

**Visual:** American Silver Eagle coin, walking liberty design
**Duration:** 24s minimum (55 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: The American Silver Eagle is the most recognized silver coin in the USA. It is also the most expensive. In normal markets you pay 15 to 25 percent over spot. During supply crunches in 2021 and 2022, that premium climbed above 50 percent. Same silver inside. Much higher cost to own it.

---

## STAT: 14 PERCENT MAPLE LEAF PREMIUM

**Visual:** Canadian Silver Maple Leaf coin
**Duration:** 20s minimum (46 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: The Canadian Silver Maple Leaf costs less than an Eagle but more than a round. Typical premiums run 10 to 18 percent over spot. They are .9999 fine silver, universally recognized worldwide, and resell easily at most dealers. The premium is the price of that recognition.

---

## CHART: PREMIUM COMPARISON

**Visual:** Horizontal bar chart showing premium by product type
**Duration:** 20s minimum (45 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: The chart tells the story. Rounds at 5 percent. Maple Leafs at 14. Eagles at 20 or higher. The metal inside every one of them is identical. What changes is the wrapper cost. Choose the wrapper based on your goal, not habit.

---

## STAT: DOLLAR GAP AT 100 OUNCES

**Visual:** Calculator, silver stack, price comparison graphic
**Duration:** 26s minimum (59 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: At a 30 dollar spot price, rounds cost about 31 to 32 dollars per ounce. Eagles cost 34 to 37 dollars. At 100 ounces that gap is 300 to 600 dollars in extra premiums for the exact same amount of silver. That is money you could have used to buy 10 more ounces of rounds instead.

---

## STAT: SAME .999 FINE SILVER

**Visual:** Purity stamp close-up, .999 marking on silver
**Duration:** 22s minimum (51 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: Both rounds and silver coins are .999 fine silver. The Canadian Maple Leaf hits .9999 fine, slightly higher purity, but the practical difference for a physical reserve is negligible. You are not getting better silver by paying a higher premium. The metal is the same. The cost is not.

---

## STAT: LIQUIDITY AND RESALE

**Visual:** Coin shop counter, dealer buying silver
**Duration:** 25s minimum (57 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: Government coins have one real advantage over rounds. Recognition. An American Silver Eagle or Canadian Maple Leaf is immediately known at any dealer in any city. Generic rounds from unfamiliar private mints can face more scrutiny. For name-brand rounds from Sunshine or SilverTowne, resale is rarely a problem. For obscure rounds, expect more questions.

---

## STAT: NUMISMATIC VALUE

**Visual:** Proof coin set, collector album
**Duration:** 25s minimum (57 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: Silver rounds have zero collector value. Their price tracks spot price and nothing else. Government coins are different. Rare American Silver Eagle proof sets and low-mintage years sell for multiples of their silver melt value. If you want numismatic upside, rounds cannot give it to you. If you only want silver, rounds are more efficient.

---

## STAT: WHICH TO BUY

**Visual:** Split screen: rounds in tube vs Eagle in capsule
**Duration:** 24s minimum (55 words / 2.5 + 2)

Narration: The answer depends on your goal. Building a reserve efficiently? Buy rounds. More silver per dollar. Gifting someone recognizable silver? Buy government coins. Collecting? Coins only, rounds have no collector upside. Most serious buyers hold both. Rounds for bulk weight. A few rolls of Eagles for liquidity and gifting purposes.
