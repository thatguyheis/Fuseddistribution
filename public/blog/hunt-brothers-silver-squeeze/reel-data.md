## stats
- text: $49.45 JAN 1980 PEAK
  explanation: The all-time nominal silver price high, reached after a 7-year accumulation
  graphic_type: growth

  narration: Silver started the 1970s at about one dollar and fifty cents an ounce. By January 18th, 1980, it hit $49.45. That's a 33-times increase in seven years. Not because demand exploded. Because two brothers from Texas decided to own it all.

- text: 100M+ OZ ACCUMULATED
  explanation: The Hunt family controlled roughly 1/3 of the world's non-government silver
  graphic_type: percent_fill
  graphic:
    value: 33
    label: Hunt Control
    remainder_label: Rest of Market
  narration: Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt, working with Arab partners, accumulated over 100 million ounces of silver through physical holdings and futures contracts. That was approximately one-third of all non-government silver in the world. No single entity had ever controlled that share of a global commodity.

- text: 1973 START AT $1.50
  explanation: The Hunts began buying when silver was at historic lows relative to inflation
  graphic_type: none
  narration: The brothers started accumulating in 1973 when silver was around $1.50 per ounce. They were ideologically convinced that paper money would collapse. They weren't wrong about inflation: the 1970s brought real currency pain. But they made the mistake of thinking they could force the outcome they expected.

- text: SILVER RULE 7 CHANGED GAME
  explanation: COMEX restricted trading to liquidation-only in January 1980, collapsing the squeeze
  graphic_type: none
  narration: In January 1980, the COMEX exchange implemented what became known as Silver Rule 7. Traders could only close existing silver positions. They could not open new long contracts. This removed the buying pressure that had driven prices up. Prices began to fall immediately.

- text: $10.80 SILVER THURSDAY
  explanation: March 27, 1980: silver fell from $21 to $10.80 in a single trading session
  graphic_type: gap
  graphic:
    a_label: Morning Open
    a_value: 21
    b_label: Day Close
    b_value: 11
    unit: $/oz
  narration: On March 27th, 1980, the Hunt brothers could not meet a $135 million margin call from their broker Bache Group. Bache liquidated their positions on the open market. Silver fell from roughly $21 to $10.80 in a single session. Brokerage firms nearly collapsed. The Federal Reserve coordinated a $1.1 billion emergency loan to prevent a broader financial crisis.

- text: $134M CIVIL SETTLEMENT
  explanation: The Hunts settled CFTC and civil charges in 1989 without criminal prosecution
  graphic_type: none
  narration: The Hunt brothers avoided criminal charges. In 1989 they settled civil claims brought by the CFTC and a Peruvian mining company called Minpeco for a combined $134 million. Nelson Hunt filed for personal bankruptcy in 1988, listing $2.5 billion in liabilities.

- text: 31 YEARS TO REVISIT $49
  explanation: Silver did not return to the 1980 nominal peak until April 2011
  graphic_type: timeline
  graphic:
    min: 1980
    max: 2011
    unit: years
    label: Return to $49
  narration: After the 1980 collapse, silver traded between $4 and $10 for most of the next two decades. The metal didn't revisit $49 until April 2011, 31 years later. That time the move was driven by real industrial demand growth from electronics, solar panels, and ETF investment. A completely different story.

- text: PHYSICAL HELD, FUTURES WIPED OUT
  explanation: Physical silver holders took losses; leveraged futures traders were wiped out entirely
  graphic_type: none
  narration: Investors holding physical silver at the 1980 peak took losses when the price collapsed. But they kept the metal. Traders who held leveraged futures contracts faced margin calls they couldn't meet and lost everything. The difference between a paper position and a physical one was the difference between a loss and a wipeout.
