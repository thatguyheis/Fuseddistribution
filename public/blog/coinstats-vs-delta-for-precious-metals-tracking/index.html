CoinStats vs Delta for Precious Metals Tracking

September 14, 2026 Silver Investing

CoinStats and Delta are useful portfolio trackers, but their official documentation describes investment and digital asset workflows rather than a dedicated physical bullion ledger. That distinction matters when you own coins or bars in a safe, a depository, or an estate box. A separate inventory can keep a dated record of ounces, cost basis, storage, and documents, while a market tracker may show an ETF or a security that gives you price exposure. Those are different records.

What CoinStats and Delta document

CoinStats describes itself as a crypto portfolio tracker. Its help center documents connected exchanges and wallets, manual portfolios, transaction entry, portfolio analytics, and allocation views. A manual portfolio lets a user enter an asset and transaction details, but the documentation does not establish a dedicated physical gold or silver inventory, vault verification, assay workflow, serial-number register, or bullion pricing feed. Do not assume that entering a ticker creates proof that a bar exists.

Delta describes itself as a read-only investment portfolio tracker. Its official help pages cover stocks, funds, ETFs, cryptocurrencies, forex, indices, broker connections, wallets, manual transactions, and following assets for price tracking. Delta does not hold or withdraw funds. Its documented categories do not establish a physical-metal inventory or a chain-of-custody record for coins and bars. A precious-metals ETF or mining share in Delta is a market security, not the same asset as allocated bullion.

A useful comparison

CoinStats is the more clearly documented choice when your workflow is transaction entry, crypto and wallet connections, and portfolio analytics. Its manual portfolio records supported digital assets and transactions. The reviewed instructions do not establish custom entries for physical coins or bars, so do not treat it as a bullion ledger. Verify the selected asset, unit, currency, date, fees, and notes. Keep the original inventory outside the app.

Delta is the more clearly documented choice when your workflow centers on stocks, ETFs, funds, broker connections, market views, and a consolidated read-only investment picture. It can help compare a silver ETF or a mining company with other securities if those instruments are supported. That comparison describes exposure to a financial instrument. It does not value, insure, authenticate, or locate physical bullion.

Neither official product source reviewed here supports a claim that the app automatically recognizes a box of coins, confirms a dealer delivery, reads a bar serial number, or maintains a vault balance. If a product search returns a metal themed security, inspect the instrument type and issuer before adding it. A symbol, name, or chart is not evidence of ownership.

Build a physical bullion record first

For coins, record an inventory ID, denomination, date, mint mark, stated fineness, weight, condition, holder, and photograph. For bars, record maker, stated fineness, weight, serial number, assay card, packaging, and storage location. Record the acquisition date, seller, invoice, fees, and whether the item is allocated, pooled, or held personally. Keep a second copy of the inventory and store invoices separately from the metal.

If the metal is inherited, record who is authorized to act before you move, sell, or divide it. Preserve the chain of custody. Ask a qualified numismatic or precious-metals appraiser about unusual coins, graded pieces, disputed ownership, or a potential sale. Do not clean coins or use an app’s performance chart as an appraisal.

Use apps as views, not as the source of truth

You can use a spreadsheet or a manual portfolio to summarize physical holdings, but label each line as physical, ETF, mining equity, or another proxy. Use separate fields for quantity, troy ounces, purchase cost, storage, and documents. If an app only accepts a market asset, do not force a physical coin into that asset merely to make the dashboard balance.

Review the record after every purchase, transfer, sale, appraisal, or change of storage. Export data when the product supports it and reconcile the export with your invoice folder. For tax basis or estate reporting, consult a tax professional. App analytics can calculate a view from the data entered; they cannot correct an incorrect identity, missing invoice, counterfeit item, or unrecorded transfer.

Questions to ask before choosing

Start by naming the thing you want to measure. If the question is “How did my ETF position perform?” a market tracker may have the right price history, corporate actions, and portfolio fields. If the question is “Which tube contains the 1964 dime recorded on my invoice?” you need an inventory with photographs and a storage location. These questions can sit beside each other, but one app record should not pretend to answer both.

Ask whether the connection is read-only, what data it imports, how often it refreshes, and what happens when an instrument is unavailable. Ask whether a manual entry supports notes, units, dates, fees, and exports. Treat every answer as a product capability only when the official documentation states it. A search result, community post, or a ticker that happens to contain the word silver is not enough evidence.

For a family collection, assign access carefully. The person who maintains the dashboard may not be the person authorized to sell the metal. Keep a dated change log when a coin moves, a bar goes to a depository, or an item is appraised. If an app account is closed or a plan changes, the independent inventory should still explain what was owned, where it was stored, and which documents support the entry.

There is also a measurement problem. Apps may display a portfolio in a currency and calculate performance from transaction prices. Physical bullion is often discussed by piece, gross weight, fine weight, condition, premium, and selling venue. Those measures are related but not interchangeable. Record the unit you actually know, label estimates, and avoid presenting an app total as an appraisal or insurance value.

Choose CoinStats when you want documented crypto and wallet connections, manual transaction entry, and analytics around a broader digital portfolio. Choose Delta when you want documented stock, ETF, fund, broker, wallet, and market tracking in a read-only investment view. Choose neither as your only physical bullion register. Keep the custody and identity record in a controlled inventory, then use an app only for the financial view it actually supports.

The product boundaries are part of the comparison. CoinStats official help documents manual portfolios and crypto centered connections. Delta official help documents read-only tracking for investments, stocks, funds, ETFs, crypto, and wallets. Neither reviewed source establishes automatic physical bullion tracking. That limit is more useful than an invented feature list when your goal is accurate precious-metals records.
