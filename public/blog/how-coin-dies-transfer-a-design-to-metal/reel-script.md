## HOOK

**Visual:** The tool carries the design
**Duration:** 19s minimum

Narration: A coin die is a tool that presses a design into a prepared metal piece. At the U.S. Mint, working dies strike coins; they are produced through a sequence involving hubs and dies. The design on the striking tool is reversed so that the finished coin receives the intended image.

## OVERLAY: NAME THE TOOL

**Visual:** Name the tool
**Duration:** 19s minimum

Narration: That distinction helps when looking at an illustration. Ask whether you are seeing the artwork, a hub, a die or the coin. Images of these stages may look related, but they are not interchangeable. A caption that identifies the stage is part of the explanation, not a decorative detail.

## OVERLAY: FOLLOW THE TRANSFER

**Visual:** Follow the transfer
**Duration:** 16s minimum

Narration: The Mint's coin production guide explains that the obverse and reverse dies press against the planchet during striking. The planchet's metal takes the design. A surrounding collar controls expansion and is part of the edge-forming process described by the Mint.

---

## OVERLAY: UNDERSTAND WORKING DIES

**Visual:** Understand working dies
**Duration:** 20s minimum

Narration: The Mint describes master tools and working tools as different generations in the process. Working hubs can produce replacement working dies as those dies wear. The master tools are preserved through more limited use. This is the Mint's process explanation, not a statement that every mint and historical period follows an identical sequence.

---

## OVERLAY: RECORD OBSERVATIONS CAREFULLY

**Visual:** Record observations carefully
**Duration:** 44s minimum

Narration: Learning about dies may make you more interested in the marks and details on a coin. Keep a visible observation separate from a claim about its cause. A photograph can document what is visible under its lighting and focus; it does not automatically explain when or how a feature formed. A useful record names the coin or inventory identifier, the view, the source of any specialist attribution and the unresolved question. If another person has identified a variety, preserve their exact report and supporting information. For a reliable explanation, cite the mint's process and keep observations about individual items in separate records. That gives you a sound starting point for learning more while leaving attribution, authenticity and value to evidence that actually addresses them.
