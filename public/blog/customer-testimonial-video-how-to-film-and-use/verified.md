# Customer Testimonial Videos: How to Film and Use Them

To make a useful customer testimonial video, record one customer's real experience, capture clear sound, and edit the answer around a specific question a potential customer has. Start with the story and the permission to use it. A better camera cannot rescue a vague answer, a misleading edit, or an unclear agreement about where the footage will appear.

You can begin with a phone, a stable support, and a quiet room. The aim is to help someone understand the experience of working with your business, not to manufacture a perfect review. This workflow takes you from choosing an interview subject to publishing an approved clip and checking how people use it. Treat suggested timings as editing choices, then confirm the rules of the platform receiving the final video.

## Choose One Customer Story for the Testimonial

Pick a customer who can describe a particular interaction in their own words. A repair visit, a completed installation, or a clearly defined service experience gives the interview a useful focus. Ask yourself which prospective customer's question the story might answer. It could concern communication, the booking process, or what happens during the service.

Do not write an outcome for the customer to repeat. If you want to discuss a measurable result, establish what happened and what records support it before including the claim. Avoid presenting an invented example as an actual customer's statement. A modest, specific account is more useful than a broad statement that your business always delivers exceptional results. Write the intended subject at the top of your filming notes so the conversation stays on that experience.

## Agree on Permission and Intended Video Use

Explain the filming plan before setting up the camera. Describe where you intend to use the clip, whether paid advertising is included, how the customer will be identified, and whether their business name or logo will appear. Record the agreed permissions and provide an opportunity to ask questions. If the situation involves employees, minors, confidential information, or sensitive services, get appropriate advice about the permissions needed.

Keep approval records with the footage rather than in a separate inbox that will be hard to search later. Offer a review of the proposed edit to catch factual mistakes and unexpected context. Do not make a positive testimonial a condition of helping with an unresolved service problem. The customer should be able to describe their experience honestly and decline participation without being pressured into a performance.

## Prepare Three Open Interview Questions

Use a short question list instead of a script containing the answers. These three prompts provide a practical starting point:

- What did you need help with before contacting the business?
- What happened during the work or service?
- What would you tell someone considering the same service?

Follow up on an interesting detail rather than steering toward a predetermined compliment. For example, ask what a clear explanation helped the customer understand, instead of telling them that communication was excellent. If they mention a result, ask what they mean and whether they are comfortable including it. Share the topics before filming so the customer can think about their experience. They do not need to memorize a polished answer or copy the language from your website.

## Set Up Clear Sound Before Improving the Picture

Choose a location away from machinery, music, conversation, and traffic noise where possible. Make a brief test recording and listen to it before the interview. Check that the customer's voice is understandable without turning the volume unusually high. If you use an external microphone, confirm the phone or camera is actually recording from it.

Position the device on a stable support and leave enough room around the speaker for later cropping. Place the camera near eye level. Use the available light to make the face visible, and avoid a bright window directly behind the customer. Keep the background relevant but uncluttered. Remove documents, screens, vehicle plates, or personal items that should not be published. A short sound and framing check can prevent discovering an avoidable problem after the customer has left.

## Record the Testimonial as a Conversation

Ask one question at a time and allow a pause before the customer answers. Let them finish rather than speaking over the last words. If an answer refers to something the viewer will not hear, ask a neutral follow-up that helps the customer explain the context. Keep the conversation focused without insisting on a particular choice of words.

It is fine to record another take when noise interrupts or the customer loses their place. Do not keep repeating a question until they make a stronger claim than they intended. Note useful moments as you go. If you record supporting shots of the location, product, or work, check permission for everything visible. These shots should explain the story, not imply that unrelated footage documents this customer's exact experience.

## Edit for Clarity Without Changing the Meaning

Choose the answer that best addresses the question you identified at the start. Remove long pauses and unrelated digressions, but keep qualifications that affect what the customer is saying. Do not join words from different answers to create a statement they did not make. Keep an unedited copy so you can compare the finished clip with the original conversation.

A simple sequence is enough: identify the customer as agreed, present the relevant experience, and end with a clear next step for the viewer. Use supporting footage only when it adds context. Review every name, service description, and stated result against your notes. If an edit makes a personal experience sound like a guarantee for everyone, revise it before asking the customer to approve the final version.

## Add Captions and Review Any Disclosures

Transcribe the spoken words, then correct automatic captions by listening to the recording. Check names, technical terms, punctuation, and timing. Preview the captions on a phone to make sure the text stays readable and does not cover the speaker's face. Keep important information away from areas that a platform's controls may cover.

For US advertising, the FTC explains that endorsements must reflect honest experiences and that material connections may need disclosure. Payment, free products, employment, or a family relationship can matter to how an audience understands a testimonial. Review the [FTC endorsement guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking) for your situation. Keep any necessary disclosure visible and understandable in the format people will actually see. Captions and a platform label are not a reason to skip reviewing the final presentation.

## Prepare a Version for Each Publishing Destination

Keep a master edit, then export versions for the places where you have permission to use the testimonial. A website player and a vertical social post may need different framing or text placement. Preview the actual upload rather than assuming the exported file will appear identically in every app. Check current format and duration requirements before scheduling.

For Google Business Profile media, Google's published guidance currently limits uploaded videos to 30 seconds. Do not confuse that media limit with separate instructions for business verification videos. Source: [Google Business Profile photo and video guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6103862?hl=en). If a destination needs a shorter cut, choose a complete thought rather than cutting off an important qualification. Preserve the meaning and required disclosures in every version, including a very short excerpt.

## Place the Video Beside a Relevant Next Step

Put the testimonial where it can help answer a reader's immediate question. On a service page, introduce the customer's experience with a short, accurate description. Make the next step easy to find, whether that is reading service details, asking a question, or requesting a booking. Do not force the viewer to search through unrelated pages to understand the offer.

Review your [contact page](/blog/contact-page-best-practices-for-local-business/) before sending visitors there. For an [email newsletter](/blog/email-newsletter-ideas-for-small-business/), use an accurate summary and link to the page containing the approved video. Keep track of which version you placed in each location. If the customer's permission or the service information changes, that record will help you identify the posts and pages that need attention.

## Measure Response Without Claiming Proof of Causation

Record the publication date, destination, and the question the video was intended to answer. Look at the measures available for that destination, such as plays, completion, visits to the linked page, and relevant inquiries. Check whether viewers can find the next step and whether the video answers the question clearly. Use what you learn to improve a later interview or edit.

An increase in calls after publication does not by itself prove that the video caused the increase. Other campaigns, seasonal changes, and unrelated business activity can affect the same measures. Keep performance claims proportionate to the evidence. Begin with one approved customer story, publish it where it fits, and use the result to refine the next recording.
