The familiar image of Abraham Lincoln on the U.S. Cent - a ubiquitous symbol of American history - didn’t simply materialize in 1909. The story of its creation, the first circulating U.S. Coin to feature a real person and president, is a surprisingly complex one, rooted in a chance encounter and a Mint’s evolving record. This article will explore the circumstances surrounding the release of the 1909 cent, commemorating Lincoln’s centennial, and trace the changes to the coin’s design over subsequent anniversaries.

The 1909 cent, released on August 2, marked a significant moment in U.S. Coinage. Victor David Brenner designed the portrait of the president, and the Mint’s records document a degree of uncertainty about the precise decision-making process. In particular, President Theodore Roosevelt was drawn to a plaque created by Brenner. Following the initial release of over 22 million cents struck in Philadelphia, the coin’s design would continue to evolve, reflecting changing commemorative events and ultimately, a shift in its reverse design.

The centennial issue reached the public in August

The 1909 cent commemorated Abraham Lincoln’s centennial, and its release occurred on August 2, 1909. This marked a significant event as it was the first circulating U.S. Coin to feature a real person and a president. The Mint recorded this public release date and documented the production of over 22 million coins in Philadelphia by July of that year. This figure represents the quantity of coins struck at the Philadelphia Mint specifically for distribution as circulating currency. The Mint’s records detail this production volume as a key aspect of the centennial issue.

The 1909 Lincoln cent’s release was a deliberate action by the Mint, signifying a milestone in U.S. Coinage. The Philadelphia Mint was responsible for the majority of the coins produced for this commemorative issue. The Mint’s documentation confirms that the production of 22 million coins was completed before the August 2 release date, indicating a coordinated effort to meet the demand for this special coin.

The decision to produce this quantity of coins was directly tied to the centennial celebration and the Mint’s role in creating a circulating coin honoring Lincoln. The Mint’s records provide a precise account of the production volume, offering a tangible measure of the 1909 Lincoln cent’s significance.

Brenner’s portrait and the limits of the record

Victor David Brenner designed the portrait that appeared on the 1909 centennial issue. The Mint’s record indicates that the precise decision process for selecting his design remains not fully documented. This suggests a degree of uncertainty surrounding the selection of Brenner’s portrait among multiple possibilities. The Mint’s documentation confirms that a plaque bearing Brenner’s name had caught the attention of President Theodore Roosevelt. This observation is recorded as a factor in the Mint’s consideration of the portrait.

The Mint’s account emphasizes that the decision to feature Lincoln on the cent was not the result of a meticulously planned, formally documented process. Instead, it appears the Mint’s record reflects a more organic development, influenced by factors such as public attention and the visual impact of Brenner’s design. The Mint’s documentation provides a crucial distinction: while the 1909 cent was the first circulating U.S. Coin to depict a real person and president - Lincoln - the specific steps leading to that decision are not fully captured in their records. This lack of a detailed, formal process highlights the somewhat spontaneous nature of the event.

The reverse design changed across later anniversaries

The reverse design of the cent evolved significantly over the decades following its initial release in 1909. The Mint’s record indicates a progression of designs, beginning with the Wheat Ears reverse, which was utilized from 1909 through 1958. This design was subsequently replaced by the Lincoln Memorial reverse, employed from 1959 through 2008. During the 2009 centennial issue, the Mint produced four distinct reverse designs to commemorate the bicentennial of American independence. These four designs were part of a larger series of commemorative reverses.

Following the completion of the 2009 designs, the Mint introduced a new reverse design featuring the Union Shield in 2010. This marked a continuation of the Mint’s practice of utilizing different reverse designs for commemorative U.S. Coins. The shift in reverse designs occurred periodically, reflecting the Mint’s approach to honoring significant anniversaries and historical events on the circulating cent.

A real person appeared on a circulating U.S. Coin

The 1909 cent broke familiar portrait practice because the Mint identifies it as the first circulating U.S. Coin to feature a real person and a president. This marked the centennial of Abraham Lincoln’s birth. The Mint records the public release of the coin as August 2, 1909, coinciding with the anniversary celebration. Prior to this date, U.S. Coins typically featured allegorical figures or symbolic representations rather than portraits of individuals.

The Mint’s documentation indicates that more than 22 million coins had been struck in Philadelphia by July of that year, demonstrating the significant scale of the commemorative issue. This shift to a portrait of a real president - Lincoln - was a deliberate choice made by the Mint to honor the centennial.

The decision process surrounding Lincoln’s appearance on the cent remains somewhat unclear in the Mint’s records. While Victor David Brenner designed the portrait, the precise rationale for this selection is not fully documented.

Ultimately, the 1909 cent stands as a unique example in U.S. Coinage history, representing the first time a real person, specifically a president, was featured on a circulating coin.
