## What to Know About State Sales Tax on Silver

Sales tax on silver coins isn’t a universal rule. It varies significantly from state to state, and even within states, depending on the type of silver and how it's sold. Generally, states tax silver bullion (bars, rounds, etc.) but often exempt collector coins. Understanding these distinctions is crucial for accurate budgeting and avoiding unexpected tax liabilities. Let’s dive into the specifics.

## States That Typically Charge Sales Tax on Silver Bullion

Several states consistently apply sales tax to silver bullion. Here’s a breakdown of some of the most common offenders:

* **California:** California is notorious for its high sales tax rates and applies sales tax to all silver bullion transactions. As of 2024, the state’s standard sales tax rate is 7.25%, and counties can add additional local taxes, potentially pushing the total rate to over 9%.
* **Texas:** Texas also levies sales tax on silver bullion. The rate varies by county, but it’s typically 6.25% plus any applicable local taxes.
* **Florida:** Florida’s sales tax rate is 6%, but this includes local surtaxes, which can increase the total rate. Silver bullion is subject to this rate.
* **New York:** New York’s sales tax rate is 4%, but like Florida, local taxes can add to the total.
* **Illinois:** Illinois’s sales tax rate is 6.25%, and it applies to silver bullion.
* **Pennsylvania:** Pennsylvania’s sales tax rate is 6%, and it applies to silver bullion.
* **Ohio:** Ohio’s sales tax rate is 5.75%, and it applies to silver bullion.
* **Washington:** Washington’s sales tax rate is 6.5%, and it applies to silver bullion.

These states generally consider silver bullion to be a commodity, and therefore subject to sales tax.

## Collector Coins: A Different Story

The [rules](/blog/irs-rules-for-selling-silver-coins-1099-b-reporting/) regarding collector coins are often more complex. Many states exempt certain types of collector coins, but the definition of “collector coin” can vary. Generally, these coins are rare, limited edition, or have historical significance. The IRS also provides guidance on what constitutes a collectible coin.

* **The IRS definition:** The IRS considers a coin to be a collectible if it’s purchased primarily for its numismatic value (its value as a collectible) rather than its intrinsic value (the value of the metal it’s made from).
* **State variations:** States interpret this definition differently. Some states require you to demonstrate that you’re purchasing the coin for its collectible value, while others simply exempt coins that meet certain criteria (e.g., are graded by a reputable grading service like PCGS or NGC).

## How Sales Tax is Calculated on Silver

When sales tax *is* applied, the calculation typically involves multiplying the purchase price of the silver by the applicable sales tax rate. For example, if you buy a 10-ounce silver bar for $1,000 in a state with a 7.25% sales tax rate, your sales tax would be $72.50.

## Getting Started: Due Diligence is Key

Before you buy silver coins, it’s essential to research the sales tax laws in the state where you’re purchasing. Here’s what you should do:

1. **Identify the State:** Determine the state where the seller is located and where you'll be taking possession of the silver.
2. **Check the State’s Tax Website:** Visit the state’s Department of Revenue website to find information on sales tax rates and exemptions.
3. **Ask the Seller:** Confirm whether the silver is subject to sales tax and, if so, what the applicable rate is.
4. **Document Everything:** Keep records of your purchase, including the price, date, and seller’s information.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

* **Assuming Silver is Tax-Exempt:** Don’t assume that silver is automatically exempt from sales tax. Always verify the rules in the specific state.
* **Misinterpreting “Investment”:** Understand how your state defines “investment” for silver bullion exemptions.
* **Ignoring Local Taxes:** Remember that some counties or cities may add additional sales taxes on top of the state rate.
* **Not Keeping Records:** Maintain accurate records of your purchases to support your tax return if needed.
