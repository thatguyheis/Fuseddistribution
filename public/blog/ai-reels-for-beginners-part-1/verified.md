AI video is easier to approach when you stop asking it to invent your business. Give a production workflow real examples, useful topics, and clear review rules. Then use AI to help shape those inputs into short videos. The first goal is not to publish everywhere. It is to make a few accurate videos that sound like your business and teach you what to improve.

This is part one of a two part guide. It covers the five foundations that make a first batch useful. [Part two explains the repeatable workflow and links the full flowchart](/blog/ai-reels-workflow-part-2/).

## Start with the work you already do

AI can help organize, script, caption, or assemble a video, but it does not know what happened at your job site, what your product looks like, or how your customers describe a problem. If those details matter, bring them in yourself and check the finished result. A system may produce fluent language that is still wrong. Treat every name, claim, price, result, and customer detail as something to verify.

Begin with photos and clips that show the real work: a finished project, a tool in use, a product from several angles, a team preparing for the day, a before and after, or a common question answered in person. A useful first collection might contain twenty or more pieces. That number is a starter exercise, not a performance benchmark. Ten clear, relevant clips are more useful than a folder full of unrelated footage.

Ask permission before filming staff, customers, private homes, license plates, paperwork, or computer screens. Remove personal information from clips. Keep notes about where an asset came from, when it can be used, and any limits on its use. If a customer has not agreed to appear in marketing, choose another visual.

## Choose topics before asking for drafts

Write down the questions people already ask before they hire you or buy from you. Add common mistakes, routine maintenance, how a service works, what happens after someone contacts you, and how to compare options. A list of thirty questions gives you a useful first month of ideas. A year long topic bank can come later, after you learn which subjects are clear and valuable.

Review the list yourself. Remove topics that expose a customer, promise a result you cannot support, need private details, or fall outside your expertise. Mark topics that need a current price, regulation, or product specification so someone checks those facts before publication. An approved list sets a boundary for production. It does not make every future sentence automatically accurate.

For each idea, write down the audience, the question, the one answer they should remember, and the next step. For example:

- Audience: a homeowner planning a roof repair.
- Question: what should I photograph before calling a contractor?
- One answer: take wide views and close views of the visible damage, without climbing onto the roof.
- Next step: save the photos and ask a qualified roofer what information they need.

This simple brief gives a writer and editor something concrete to check. It also prevents a short video from trying to teach five lessons at once.

## Build a labeled B roll library

B roll is supporting footage that plays while the narration explains a point. Store it in a folder that is easy to search. Use plain labels such as `roof inspection`, `crew preparing tools`, or `customer pickup`. Add a short note when the clip needs permission, should only be used for a particular service, or must not show identifying details.

Keep the library factual. A clip of one project should not be presented as another customer's job. A stock scene should not imply that it shows your team or your work. If you use stock footage, record its source and license. If you use an illustration, call it an illustration when viewers might mistake it for evidence.

Reuse makes production simpler, but context still matters. A clip can be technically approved and still look wrong under a specific sentence. Review each edit with the narration playing. Replace footage that suggests a claim the words do not support.

## Pick one recognizable voice

Choose whether the videos use your recorded voice, a permitted voice replica, or one consistent narration voice. Keep the choice steady while you test the rest of the workflow. If you use a person's voice, get their permission and explain where the recording may appear. Keep the original recordings and the approval record in a restricted folder.

Listen to every final voice track. Check names, numbers, pronunciation, missing words, and pauses. Captions should match what is actually said. Viewers may watch without sound, so use captions that are large enough to read and do not cover the subject.

## Treat posting frequency as a choice to test

The supplied guide suggests posting twice a day for maximum reach. Do not present that as a rule that guarantees more views. YouTube's current Shorts guidance says creators should choose a consistent, sustainable schedule and says there is no minimum posting cadence required for Shorts to perform well. It recommends learning what works for the audience and the creator's own capacity. See [YouTube's Shorts upload schedule tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13616979) and [Shorts discovery guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11914225).

Choose a schedule you can maintain while keeping review quality high. Start with one clear home platform, then adapt the video for other places you serve customers. Check the crop, text, audio rights, captions, and link on each platform. Do not assume that an upload order creates a distribution advantage.

## Make three drafts before scaling

Try a small test batch. Use three topics from the approved list and the same core workflow. Review each draft for factual accuracy, clear audio, readable captions, on brand language, useful pacing, and visuals that fit the narration. Ask one person who resembles your customer whether the point was clear. Fix the largest issue before making more.

Measure the videos with the platform's own analytics. On YouTube, official Shorts analytics include views, shown in feed, likes, comments, subscribers, and other discovery information. Retention and watch time help show whether people stayed with the video. Compare the result with your own typical posts, not with an unrelated viral clip. See [YouTube's Shorts analytics guide](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217).

Keep a note of what changed between versions. Was the opening clearer? Did the example help? Was one clip confusing? Change one important element at a time so the review can teach you something. Early results are feedback, not proof that one format will always win.

## Your first week checklist

1. Collect and label a small set of real work photos and clips. Start with twenty if you have them, but keep only useful, permission cleared material.
2. Write thirty customer questions and approve the first three topics you want to answer.
3. Make three draft reels, review every word and frame, and write down one improvement before publishing more.

You do not need to automate every step on the first day. First prove that your inputs are clear, your review catches mistakes, and your finished video represents the business honestly. Then make the repeatable process in [part two](/blog/ai-reels-workflow-part-2/) easier to run.
