George Washington Carver joined Tuskegee’s faculty in 1896 and spent 47 years there teaching agricultural science and promoting crop rotation to restore depleted soil. His work began immediately upon his arrival at the institution. Carver’s primary focus was developing sustainable farming practices for the South’s agricultural landscape. He implemented crop rotation techniques, a system where different crops are grown in a planned sequence on the same land. This was crucial because Southern soils had been severely damaged by years of cotton cultivation, leading to a decline in fertility. Carver’s approach aimed to revitalize these soils by alternating crops, enriching the land and preventing the buildup of harmful elements. 

Carver’s methods were particularly relevant to the challenges faced by Southern farmers. The repeated planting of cotton had exhausted the soil’s nutrients, making it difficult to sustain yields. Carver’s crop rotation strategies provided a practical solution, offering farmers a way to maintain productivity and improve the long-term health of their land. This work was central to his role at Tuskegee and contributed significantly to the advancement of agricultural science in the region.
