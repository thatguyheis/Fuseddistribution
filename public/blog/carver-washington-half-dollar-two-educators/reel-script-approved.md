# Reel Script: Why the Carver-Washington Half Dollar Honors Two Educators
format: long-form
content-kind: educate

---
## HOOK
Narration: This half dollar pairs two educators. Booker T. Washington built Tuskegee’s school; George Washington Carver spent decades teaching farmers. Why did Congress place their names together on one coin?

---
## OVERLAY: WASHINGTON BUILDS TUSKEGEE
Narration: Booker T. Washington was born enslaved in Virginia in 1856. After emancipation, he worked and studied, then attended Hampton Institute. In 1881 he arrived in Tuskegee, Alabama, to lead a school that began with thirty students meeting in a church. Students helped build the campus, and the school taught practical trades as well as academic subjects.

---
## OVERLAY: CARVER TEACHES AGRICULTURAL SCIENCE
Narration: In 1896, Washington recruited Carver to direct Tuskegee’s agriculture department. Carver had studied at Iowa State Agricultural College, where he earned a master’s degree. At Tuskegee he worked for forty-seven years. His teaching addressed depleted Southern soil: crop rotation could reduce reliance on cotton and help maintain fertility. He also taught alternative crops and farming methods to students and farmers.

---
## OVERLAY: CONGRESS JOINS THEIR NAMES
Narration: The coin did not appear during their lifetimes. Congress first authorized a Booker T. Washington half dollar in 1946, then amended the program in 1951 to include Carver. The Mint produced the combined Carver-Washington issue from 1951 through 1954. Its reverse shows an outline map of the United States, while the obverse places both profiles and names together.

---
## OVERLAY: HATHAWAY’S DESIGN AND THE 1954 ENDING
Narration: The law assigned distribution to two memorial organizations tied to their birthplaces. It was the Mint’s last commemorative coin until the modern program began in 1982.

---
## OVERLAY: TWO EDUCATORS ON ONE COIN
Narration: The coin is a small record of a larger partnership: Washington built an institution, Carver taught agricultural science through it, and a later generation placed both names on one national object.
