# How to Summarize Your Own Document With AI

## Begin with a document you are allowed to share

Can AI summarize a document without changing its meaning? It can help you make a first draft, but you still need to check the summary against the source. Begin with a document you have permission to use and that contains no information you should keep private. A public policy, product guide, or sanitized procedure is a safer first test than a customer file or personnel record.

ChatGPT can work with common uploaded formats such as PDF, DOCX, TXT, Markdown, and spreadsheets, although available formats and limits depend on the plan and workspace. Check the [current file upload guide](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8555545-uploading-files-and-audio-to-chatgpt) before choosing a file. If a file contains material outside the task, make a clean copy and remove it first.

## Decide who will use the summary

A useful summary depends on its purpose. A business owner scanning a procedure may need actions and exceptions. A new employee may need a plain-language overview and terms to learn. Someone preparing for a meeting may need decisions, dates, and open questions. State the reader and use before asking for a summary.

Without that direction, a model may produce a generic recap that hides the part you care about. Decide the length and shape too. You might ask for five bullet points, a short executive summary, or a table with “requirement,” “source section,” and “follow-up.” Be specific enough for someone else to review the result.

## Ask for source references and uncertainty

Try this prompt: “Summarize the attached procedure for a new team member in six bullets. Keep every deadline and required approval. For each bullet, name the section heading it came from. Put unclear or conflicting instructions in a separate list. Do not add advice that is not in the document.”

The section reference is a checking aid, not proof that the statement is accurate. Open the cited heading and compare the wording. If the model cannot identify a location, ask it to mark that point as unverified rather than supply a guess.

OpenAI explains that ChatGPT can make factual errors and may sound confident while doing so. Its [accuracy guidance](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8313428-does-chatgpt-tell-the-truth) recommends checking important information against reliable sources. Treat a summary as an index into the document, not as a replacement for the document when exact wording matters.

## Compare important details with the original

Check names, dates, quantities, conditions, and exceptions first. These details often change what a reader should do. Then check whether the summary preserved warnings and limits instead of presenting only the normal case. If a document says “ask a manager when the record is missing,” a summary that says “complete the form” has removed a stopping rule.

For a long policy, review the most important sections one at a time. Ask the model to produce a summary for one named section, then compare it with the source. You can also ask for a table of claims and locations, but verify each row yourself. If the source is scanned or poorly formatted, extraction can be incomplete, so do not assume every page was read correctly.

## Keep the summary separate from the source

Store the summary with a label showing that it is a draft and include the document title, version, date, or link. Keep the original as the source of truth. If the source changes, update the summary and check it again. Do not let an old summary silently become the current instruction.

If another AI tool or coworker will use the result, pass along the source name and the sections that still need review. Different tools do not necessarily share chat history or saved files. ## A practical review checklist

## Walk through a fictional procedure

Imagine a made-up shop procedure has three short sections: weekday hours, delivery handling, and holiday changes. It says the front door opens at 9 a.m. on weekdays, a staff member must contact the manager before accepting an after-hours delivery, and holiday hours are posted separately each month. These are fictional example rules, not a real business policy.

For a new employee, ask for a short checklist with one item per section and the section name beside each item. Then compare the output with the three original headings. Does it keep the 9 a.m. time? Does it preserve the manager approval before an after-hours delivery? Does it tell the reader to check the monthly holiday notice instead of inventing a fixed holiday schedule? Those differences affect what the reader may do, so they deserve a direct source check.

A summary that says “accept deliveries during business hours” may sound sensible, but it would not preserve the example’s after-hours stop rule. A summary that lists “holiday hours are 9 to 5” would add a schedule that the fictional source never gave. Ask the tool to show its section references, then open those sections yourself. If the references do not point to the right sentences, correct the summary or mark the statement unresolved.

## Preserve versions and handoffs

Give a summary a visible label, such as “Draft summary of Staff Opening Procedure, version 3, reviewed November 10.” The exact date and version should come from the source you used. If the original procedure changes, treat the old summary as stale until someone compares it again. Keep the file or link to the source beside the summary so a coworker can verify a detail without searching through old chats.

When handing the work to another person or AI tool, pass the source name, the summary’s status, and any points that still need checking. Do not assume the next tool can see the same attachment or conversation. If the material includes confidential information, follow your workplace rules and the service’s current data controls before sending it. You can also summarize a sanitized excerpt instead of the entire file.

These steps make a summary easier to audit without pretending it has become an approved policy. The document remains authoritative; the summary is a navigation aid. For a repeat task, write down who is allowed to create the draft, who checks source references, and where the approved version is stored. That simple handoff avoids a polished draft quietly replacing the current instructions.

Before sharing a generated summary, ask:

- Does each key statement match the original?
- Are names, dates, numbers, and exceptions preserved?
- Are uncertain points labeled instead of guessed?
- Is the summary clearly tied to the correct source version?
- Can the next reader find the referenced section?

If a check fails, correct the summary or return to the source. Do not quietly add a missing fact unless you can verify it. For a high-impact policy, have the responsible person review the output before anyone acts on it.

## Start with one safe document

Pick a short, non-sensitive document and define the reader and purpose. Ask for a concise summary with source headings, then verify the key points. Note what the model omitted or misunderstood. That gives you a realistic picture of where a summary saves time and where human review still matters.

The best first result is not the most polished paragraph. It is a summary that helps someone locate the right information while preserving the limits in the original. Which document would you test first?
