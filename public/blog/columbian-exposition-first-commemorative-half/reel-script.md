# Reel Script: How the 1892 Columbian Half Dollar Helped Prepare a World’s Fair
format: long-form
content-kind: educate

---
## HOOK
Narration: The first U.S. commemorative coin was authorized to help prepare a world’s fair. The 1892 Columbian half dollar connects coinage to the work of opening Chicago’s exposition.

---
## OVERLAY: A WORLD’S FAIR NEEDED FUNDING
Narration: The World’s Columbian Exposition was planned to open in Chicago in 1893, one year after the half dollar was authorized. Organizers still needed to fund the work of preparing the fairgrounds.

---
## OVERLAY: CONGRESS TIED COINAGE TO FAIR COSTS
Narration: On August 5, 1892, Congress authorized up to five million silver half dollars. That figure was a legal ceiling, not a statement that every possible coin was struck. The law directed the Mint to use uncurrent subsidiary silver already held by the Treasury. It allowed the coins to be paid to the exposition for labor, materials, and services used to prepare the fair, and required documented finances and guarantees for remaining costs.

---
## OVERLAY: THE COIN’S DESIGNS TRACE ITS PURPOSE
Narration: The law shaped the coin’s purpose. It was legal tender and the first commemorative coin produced by the Mint. The obverse showed Columbus in profile; the reverse showed the Santa Maria with two globes. Charles Barber designed the obverse and George Morgan the reverse.

---
## OVERLAY: A 1892 DATE FOR A 1893 EXPOSITION
Narration: The date can be confusing: the first halves were struck in 1892, while the exposition opened in 1893. Congress authorized them during the final preparation period. The Mint later issued another fair-related coin, the Queen Isabella quarter, in 1893.

---
## OVERLAY: THE LAW IS PART OF THE STORY
Narration: The Columbian half dollar records Congress using familiar coinage for a new public purpose. Its designs point to the event; its law explains why it was made.
