Give AI a Clear Video Brief Before Asking for a Script

November 13, 2026 AI Tools Video

Audience → Who has the problem?

Problem → What needs to change?

Result → What can viewers check?

Evidence → What must they see?

A vague request can produce a polished script that has no clear viewer or evidence. Before asking AI for a video, write a compact brief. Name the audience, one problem, the promised result, and what the viewer should see. This turns a broad idea into a reviewable production task.

Play the Fused reel here.

Start with one audience

The audience is not “everyone who uses AI.” Choose a real role for the fictional example: a small shop owner, a service coordinator, or a solo consultant. The choice changes the language, the problem, and the evidence. A brief that says “for a small repair shop owner” is easier to inspect than one that says “for businesses.”

Name one problem

A useful problem has a visible boundary. For example: “A repair shop receives an estimate request with no measurements or requested date.” The video can then show what to ask next. It should not claim that AI solved a real customer case. The example is a demonstration that keeps private information out of the draft.

Define one promised result

The result should be something the viewer can check. “Leave with a reviewable request checklist” is safer than “get more customers.” The first is a deliverable. The second is a business outcome that would need evidence, context, and time. Keep the promise small enough for one reel.

List the evidence to show

Tell the script what the viewer should see: a fictional request, three missing fields, a short draft, and a human review step. This prevents the visuals from becoming decoration. If a scene says “confirm the date,” show a labeled date field or a conceptual card. If the real interface is unavailable, label the visual as illustrative.

TikTok for Business describes a hook, body, and close structure. Use it as a clean outline for the brief: the hook names the problem, the body shows the method, and the close gives one action. It does not guarantee attention or conversions.

Ask for a scene plan with the script

A video brief should request both narration and a scene map. For each beat, ask for the spoken point, visible evidence, and a review question. That makes it easier to catch a mismatch such as narration about a date field over a generic office photo. It also gives an editor a reason to change the scene.

Captions matter here too. W3C guidance on prerecorded media is a reminder to treat words and timing as production inputs. Ask the script to keep sentences concise, then inspect the actual captions against the narration after synthesis. Do not treat a transcript file as proof that the final encoded video is readable.

Keep the graphic explanatory

A simple flow can show brief to script to scene. Keep it conceptual and label the fictional example. Do not add invented market sizes or performance percentages to make the graphic feel authoritative. When a number matters, record its source, unit, and period. Otherwise, use a checklist or flow.

Review the promise before production

Read the hook, early payoff, body, and CTA as one chain. The approved opening is “Before you ask AI for a video, give it this.” The early payoff answers immediately: give AI an audience, one problem, a promised result, and evidence to show. The rest of the reel demonstrates that brief.

YouTube’s retention report can help you inspect where viewers stayed or left after enough data is available. Use those moments to decide what to review, not to claim that one brief caused a result. Compare similar videos and change one creative variable at a time.

Finish with one question

The close should ask one useful question: “What problem should your next video solve?” It invites the viewer to define a bounded task. Do not stack a comment request with like, follow, share, and click requests. One action keeps the lesson aligned with the brief.

A compact brief does not replace judgment. It makes judgment visible. Give AI a person, a problem, a result, and evidence to show. Then review whether the script and scenes actually keep that promise.

Read the chart honestly

Compare two fictional requests. “Make a video about AI” supplies one field: a topic. The worked brief supplies four fields: audience, problem, result, and evidence. The bar chart counts those fields, one versus four. It does not measure model performance, content quality, leads, or views. Use the comparison to inspect whether enough instructions were supplied, not to make a marketing claim.

This review method is an editorial example supported by the production guidance below. It is a way to organize a review, not a promise of better business results.
