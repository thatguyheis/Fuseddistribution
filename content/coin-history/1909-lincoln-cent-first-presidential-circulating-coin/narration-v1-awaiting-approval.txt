For more than a century, American circulating coins avoided portraits of real people. Then, in 1909, Abraham Lincoln appeared on the cent. Why did a familiar denomination become the place for this major break with tradition?

The timing was deliberate. The new cent marked the hundredth anniversary of Lincoln’s birth. The Mint’s timeline records its release on August 2, 1909, with Lincoln shown in profile. Victor David Brenner designed the portrait that would remain on the cent’s obverse through later changes to the reverse.

Before the Lincoln cent, the penny’s designs used symbolic figures and images. The Mint says Liberty appeared on the cent for more than sixty years before the Indian Head design period, which ran from 1859 to 1909. Putting a president on an everyday coin changed what Americans encountered in their pockets and purses: a named historical person replaced an allegorical figure.

The decision also sat within a wider design conversation. President Theodore Roosevelt had pushed for stronger artistic quality in U.S. coinage. The Mint’s history notes that Roosevelt and the Treasury Department decided to honor Lincoln’s centennial with the new cent, though the agency’s record did not explain every step behind choosing that denomination and portrait. The Mint’s historical account says Victor David Brenner created the design and that the cent was officially released in August.

The reverse tells its own timeline. Two wheat ears framed the words ONE CENT from 1909 through 1958. The Lincoln Memorial followed from 1959 to 2008, then the Mint issued four different reverses during the 2009 bicentennial year. The obverse portrait stayed, while the image on the other side changed to mark different parts of Lincoln’s life.

The front made a president part of ordinary circulation; the reverse let each generation reinterpret what the cent could commemorate. A small copper colored coin became a long running public timeline.
