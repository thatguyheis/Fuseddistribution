A new coin was authorized just thirty-eight days after President Kennedy was killed. How did the Mint turn a national memorial into a half dollar that would also mark a change in American money?

The story begins on November 22, 1963. Within days, Mint Director Eva Adams contacted the Philadelphia Mint about placing Kennedy on a silver coin. Officials considered the quarter, half dollar and dollar. The half dollar was chosen, which meant replacing the Franklin design before its usual design term had run its course.

Gilroy Roberts, the Mint’s chief sculptor, adapted a portrait already prepared for a Kennedy medal. Frank Gasparro designed the reverse with the presidential coat of arms. Jacqueline Kennedy offered suggestions on the portrait, and Mint engravers prepared trial pieces for review. The authorization became law on December 30, 1963.

The schedule was unusually tight. Proof dies arrived on January 2, 1964. Denver began regular production on January 30, and Philadelphia followed the next week. On February 11, both mints held ceremonies for the new coin. That sequence shows how a coin design depends on more than an artist’s drawing: officials had to approve it, engravers had to prepare working dies, and two facilities had to begin production.

The first Kennedy half dollars were struck in 90 percent silver. That composition did not last long. A growing silver shortage led Congress to change the metal content of circulating dimes, quarters and half dollars in 1965. The replacement half dollar used a silver bearing outer layer and a copper nickel core, bringing its overall silver content to 40 percent.

So the Kennedy half dollar carries two connected stories. Its portrait records a rapid national act of remembrance. Its metal records a separate pressure on the coinage system, as the United States changed how it made everyday money.
