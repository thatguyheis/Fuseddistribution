America melted millions of silver dollars, then bought silver to replace them. Why destroy money only to make it again?

The answer connects a Morgan dollar to World War One, British India, and a shortage of silver thousands of miles from an American mint.

In 1918, Britain urgently needed silver to redeem paper rupee currency in India. The United States had a large supply sitting in Treasury vaults, already made into silver dollars. Turning those coins into bullion could help meet that demand.

On April twenty-third, Congress approved the Pittman Act. It authorized the Treasury to melt as many as 350 million silver dollars. That figure was the legal ceiling, not the number actually melted.

The law allowed the silver to be sold as bullion, and Britain became a major buyer for the government of India. American coins could therefore serve a very different purpose overseas: supplying metal for another country's currency system.

But Congress also required replacement. For silver sold under the act, the government was directed to buy newly mined American silver at one dollar per fine ounce, in sufficient quantity to replace the dollars involved.

That price referred to the silver's weight, not the face value stamped on a coin. The policy linked wartime bullion sales with future purchases from American mines.

The replacement requirement helped bring the Morgan dollar back in 1921. Its previous production had ended in 1904. Philadelphia, San Francisco, and, for the first time, Denver struck the design that year.

Later in 1921, the Peace dollar began replacing it.

So the Morgan's final year tells a bigger story than a familiar portrait returning to a coin. Silver moved from American vaults into an international wartime currency problem, and a promise to replace it brought the presses back to work.
