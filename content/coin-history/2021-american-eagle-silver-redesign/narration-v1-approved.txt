For thirty-five years, one reverse stayed on the American Silver Eagle. In 2021 it changed, while the familiar Liberty remained. What prompted the shift, and what did the Mint keep?

The program began in 1986. Its obverse uses Adolph A. Weinman’s Walking Liberty, first seen on a 1916 half dollar. The original reverse, by John Mercanti, showed a heraldic eagle with a shield, an olive branch, and arrows.

For the program’s thirty-fifth anniversary, Emily Damstra designed a new reverse: an eagle descending toward its nest with an oak branch. Mint medallic artist Michael Gaudioso sculpted her design. Instead of another heraldic arrangement, the image shows an eagle in motion.

The obverse also received careful attention. Mint artists returned to Weinman’s historical source artwork. On the 2021 coin, details in Liberty’s flag and the sun’s rays were adjusted, and Weinman’s traditional artist mark was restored. The update aimed for a more faithful rendering of the 1916 design while keeping the image collectors knew.

The Mint paired the old and new reverses in a 2021 two-coin reverse-proof set. West Point struck the original-reverse coin; San Francisco struck the new-reverse coin. Both contain 99.9 percent silver. Side by side, the set makes the transition easy to see.

That comparison tells a two-part story: the obverse looks back to Weinman’s 1916 design, while the reverse moves from Mercanti’s heraldic eagle to Damstra’s landing eagle. The Silver Eagle changed its reverse after a quarter-century, but retained the Liberty that anchored the series from the start.
