This fort shipped furs to London and lumber to Hawaii. A century after its founding, it appeared on an American half dollar. What made Fort Vancouver so important?

In 1825, the Hudson’s Bay Company established the fort beside the Columbia River, in what is now Washington. It became the headquarters and supply depot for a vast network of trading posts.

Here is how it worked. Food and trade goods went out to smaller posts. Furs came back by canoe, boat and horse brigades, then left aboard company ships bound for London. Fort Vancouver connected inland trade to ocean shipping.

And the business went well beyond fur. Farms fed the operation. A gristmill processed grain, and a sawmill produced lumber. By 1828, lumber and smoked salmon were being exported regularly to Honolulu. This wooden fort was part of a Pacific trading network.

The people made that system work. Indigenous people, Métis, French Canadians, Hawaiians and others lived and worked here. Families in the village outside the stockade supported the fort’s farms, workshops and transport operations. The story reaches far beyond the walls shown on the coin.

Those walls also changed location. The first fort stood on a bluff. In 1829, it was rebuilt on the plain below. Today’s reconstructed fort stands on that later site.

Then the border changed the business. The Oregon Treaty of 1846 placed the fort inside United States territory. The U.S. Army established Vancouver Barracks nearby in 1849. The company left Fort Vancouver in 1860.

In 1925, the United States commemorated the fort’s hundredth anniversary with this half dollar. John McLoughlin, its chief factor, appears on one side; a frontiersman and the fort appear on the other.

A small silver coin remembers a place where river trade, ocean shipping and changing borders met.
