Why is a British fur trading company named on an American half dollar? Look at our coin photograph. A frontiersman stands before a fort, while the Hudson's Bay Company appears in the lettering. This is the Fort Vancouver Centennial half dollar. The explanation begins a hundred years before this coin was issued.

In eighteen twenty-five, the London based Hudson's Bay Company established Fort Vancouver as the headquarters of its interior fur trade. The location was on the north bank of the Columbia River, in what is now Washington. These illustrated timber walls suggest a trading post. They are not an exact reconstruction of the original fort.

The real place was more than a wall around a few buildings. It served a network of trading posts, with workers including carpenters, blacksmiths, bakers, trappers and traders. The village could grow to hundreds of people when fur brigades arrived. That wider community gives the small scene on the coin a much larger story.

Now look at the portrait side in our photograph. The name below the bust identifies Doctor John McLoughlin. He was the fort's chief factor, responsible for its operations, from eighteen twenty-five to eighteen forty-five. Beside him, eighteen twenty-five and nineteen twenty-five mark the hundred year anniversary. The holder label reads P-C-G-S, M-S sixty-three.

The United States Mint credits Laura Gardin Fraser with both coin designs. It also records a surprising production detail: the coins were made in San Francisco without a mint mark. That is documented history, not a hidden letter we are claiming to see. Read the design from the portrait to the fort, then follow the lettering.

The company founded the fort this coin remembers, a hundred years earlier. That is the connection. Would you display the portrait or the fort side?
